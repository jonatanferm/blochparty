Spins to Image

How an MRI scanner turns spinning protons into a picture. Switch on the magnet, add gradients, tip the protons with a radio pulse, record their signal into k-space, then Fourier-transform it into an image.

What is simplified

  • Alignment is drawn hugely exaggerated. At 3 T only about 10 protons in a million are net aligned; that tiny excess is all the signal there is.
  • Time is slowed. Gradient-driven precession runs 40,000 times slower than real; the Larmor spin in the lab frame is slowed about 108 times and not on the same scale.
  • Relaxation (T1, T2) is sped up to a few seconds so you can watch it, and each k-space line starts from fully relaxed protons.
  • Each arrow is the net magnetization of a small volume, drawn for one in every few voxels. One ideal receive coil, no noise, an ideal slice profile, no fat, flow or field errors. For all of that, see the Bloch Party simulator itself.

1Main magnet

Each proton is a tiny magnet. With no field they point every which way. Turn up B₀ and a small majority lines up with it, and every proton spins around the field at the Larmor frequency f₀ = γ̄·B₀.

2Gradients

3RF pulse

A radio pulse at the protons' own frequency tips them over. With a gradient on, only protons whose frequency matches the pulse respond, which is how a scanner picks one slice.

4Fill k-space

Each line repeats the same recipe: tip the protons, use Gy and Gx to twist them into a spatial wave, then record while Gx sweeps the wave finer and finer. The signal says how much of that wave the object contains. In 3D, a Gz step also twists the wave along z, so one scan of the whole slab separates every slice.

5Reconstruct

Every k-space point is the strength of one wave. Adding all the waves back together, an inverse Fourier transform, gives the picture. Try it half-way through: missing lines blur the image.
